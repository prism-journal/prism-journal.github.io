What an electric car is worth depends on where it is plugged in — and on who is counting

Grid carbon intensity varies 5.1-fold across China's provinces. Whether that changes the climate case for an electric car depends on an accounting choice that papers rarely state.

Albert Fei1

1Shanghai High School International Division, Shanghai, China|PRISM 1, 3 (2026)

30 provinces 5 figures MEE/NBS official data PRISM-1-003

Abstract

Background. Electric vehicles are routinely described as roughly 40 % cleaner than petrol cars in China. That figure is a national average over grids that differ enormously: the official 2023 emission factors span 133 gCO2/kWh in Yunnan to 680 in Tianjin. A battery electric vehicle also begins life owing about 5.4 t of CO2 more than a petrol car, from manufacturing, and repays that debt only as it is driven.

Methods. Using the Ministry of Ecology and Environment's official 2023 provincial electricity emission factors, I computed the distance each of 30 provinces requires before a BEV has repaid its manufacturing debt. The calculation was run under two emission-factor conventions — combustion-only, as the Ministry publishes, and life-cycle, constructed per province from published per-technology factors and validated against the national life-cycle figure to within 1.5 % — and under nine sets of vehicle assumptions.

Findings. No province is dirty enough for a BEV never to repay: that would require a grid above 1155 gCO2/kWh and the worst province is 680. Under combustion factors every province repays within 6.3 years. Under life-cycle factors the range stretches to 3.1–10.2 years, and 17 of 30 provinces have not repaid by the end of a typical six-year ownership. The convention alone therefore decides whether most of China sees a benefit before the first owner sells. But that verdict is not robust: across plausible vehicle assumptions the count of provinces failing the six-year test moves between 0 and 26.

Conclusion. The headline reversal I set out to find does not exist. What exists is a weaker and more awkward result: per-province comparisons of this kind are under-determined by their own methods, and a paper that does not state its factor convention and its vehicle assumptions has not reported a finding.

1. Introduction

China passed 50 % new-energy-vehicle share of new passenger car sales in 2025. The environmental case usually offered for that transition is a single number: battery electric vehicles emit roughly 37–40 % less than equivalent petrol cars over their lifetime.

That number is a national average, and China's electricity is not national. Provincial grids run from hydro-dominated Yunnan and Sichuan to coal-dominated Shanxi and Inner Mongolia. If the average conceals a wide spread, then a buyer in Chengdu and a buyer in Tianjin are not making the same environmental decision, and no public guidance tells them so.

There is a second reason the average may mislead. A BEV is not born clean. Battery manufacturing means it starts about 5.4 t of CO2 in debt relative to a petrol car, and repays that debt only through use. How long repayment takes depends on the grid it charges from. This paper asks whether that repayment period differs enough between provinces to change the answer, and finds that the more consequential variable is not the province at all.

2. Data and methods

2.1 Emission factors

Provincial electricity emission factors are taken from the announcement issued jointly by the Ministry of Ecology and Environment and the National Bureau of Statistics, covering 2023 and all 30 mainland provinces1. These are official statistics from the bodies that compile them, and they are combustion-only: they count fuel burnt at the power station and exclude fuel extraction, fugitive methane, fuel transport, plant construction and transmission losses.

Because that boundary is a choice rather than a fact, a second, life-cycle factor was built for each province. Zhang et al. report life-cycle factors by generation technology2: 997.6 gCO2e/kWh for thermal generation against roughly 10–19 for hydro, wind, solar and nuclear. Non-thermal generation emits almost nothing at the point of combustion, so a province's official factor is close to its thermal share multiplied by the thermal combustion factor. Recovering that share gives each province its own life-cycle factor:

thermal_share = MEE_factor / 829          # gCO2/kWh, Chinese coal fleet
lifecycle     = (thermal_share × 997.6
                 + (1 − thermal_share) × 15) / (1 − 0.0454)

The final division applies 2023 transmission and distribution losses of 4.54 %, since a charger draws more electricity than reaches the battery. This construction is not assumed to be correct: applied to the national factor of the year Zhang et al. studied, it reproduces their published national life-cycle figure to within 1.5 %, which is the only check available and is reported here rather than omitted.

This matters because an earlier version of this analysis used a single national uplift of 1.55, inferred by comparing values from different years and methods. That figure was not sourced, and it produced a conclusion — six provinces in which a BEV never repays — that the properly constructed factors do not support. The result is recorded here because it shows how much of the answer rests on this one choice.

2.2 Vehicles

A compact BEV consuming 15 kWh/100 km is compared with a compact petrol car consuming 7.5 L/100 km, at 2310 gCO2 per litre3. The manufacturing debt of 5.4 t is the difference between 14.6 t for a BEV and 9.2 t for an equivalent petrol car4. Breakeven distance is

breakeven = 5,400,000 g ÷ (ICE_gCO2/km − BEV_gCO2/km)

and is undefined — the debt is never repaid — when the BEV emits at least as much per kilometre. Distances are expressed in years at 12,000 km/yr. Because all three vehicle parameters are single point estimates, the whole analysis is repeated across nine assumption sets in Section 3.3.

3. Results

3.1 No province is dirty enough for a BEV never to repay

A BEV fails to repay only where its per-kilometre emissions match the petrol car's 173 gCO2/km, which at 15 kWh/100 km requires a grid above 1155 gCO2/kWh. The dirtiest Chinese province, Tianjin, is 680 — nowhere near. Under combustion factors the breakeven range across all 30 provinces is 35,200–75,700 km, or 2.9–6.3 years. Breakeven rises non-linearly with grid intensity, so the gap between a clean and a dirty province widens faster than the grids themselves differ (Figure 1), and the resulting geography is shown in Figure 2.

The hypothesis this study was designed to test was that some provinces would show a reversal. They do not. That is reported as the first result rather than omitted.

3.2 The accounting convention decides the answer for most of China

Under life-cycle factors the picture changes. Breakeven stretches to 37,000–122,000 km, or 3.1–10.2 years, and Tianjin's ten years exceeds the roughly six years a Chinese owner keeps a new car. Seventeen of the thirty provinces have not repaid the debt by the time the first owner sells — against none under the official factors (Figure 3).

The gap between the two dots for a province is not noise. It is the same car, in the same province, in the same year, differing only in where an analyst drew the boundary — and for more than half of China it moves the verdict across the line that matters to a buyer.

3.3 The verdict is not robust

That result should not be reported without the following, which substantially undercuts it. Repeating the analysis across plausible vehicle assumptions moves the count of provinces failing the six-year test from 0 to 26 of 30 (Figure 5). A BEV at 13 kWh/100 km gives 9; at 18 kWh/100 km, 24. A petrol car at 9.0 L/100 km gives zero; at 6.5 L/100 km, 26. Plotting the whole parameter space (Figure 4) shows why: the six-year contour runs directly through the middle of the province distribution, so small changes in the assumed consumption sweep it across most of China.

The span produced by the analyst's choices is therefore wider than the span produced by China's geography. Any single province-level verdict of this kind is contingent on assumptions that are usually stated once in a methods section and then not revisited.

4. Discussion

Three things follow. First, the popular framing — that an electric car might be worse than a petrol car on a coal grid — is not true in China today. The threshold exists at 1155 gCO2/kWh and no province approaches it.

Second, the weaker claim is true and is the one worth making: in a majority of provinces, on life-cycle accounting, the climate benefit arrives after the first owner has sold the car. That matters for policy, because purchase subsidies are paid to first owners while much of the benefit accrues to second and third.

Third, and most usefully, the comparison is under-determined. A study can reach almost any conclusion within the plausible range by choosing a factor convention and a consumption figure. The remedy is not more studies but a reporting convention: state the boundary, state the assumed consumption, and publish the sensitivity. This paper's own headline moves from 0 to 26 provinces under that treatment, and would have been misleading without it.

5. Limitations

The life-cycle factors are constructed, not measured. They rest on one published set of per-technology factors and on the assumption that a province's official factor is close to its thermal share times a single national thermal combustion factor; provinces differ in coal quality and plant efficiency, which this ignores. Vehicle parameters are point estimates for a compact car and do not represent the fleet. The manufacturing debt is a single figure carried from one lifecycle assessment and is itself contested. Charging losses and battery degradation are not modelled, both of which would lengthen breakeven. Nothing here addresses the local air pollutants — PM2.5 and SO2 — which electrification relocates rather than eliminates; that question motivated this study and remains open.

6. Conclusion

Across 30 Chinese provinces a battery electric vehicle always repays its manufacturing carbon debt, but takes between three and ten years to do so, and on life-cycle accounting most provinces do not reach that point before the car changes hands. The comparison is nevertheless more sensitive to the analyst's assumptions than to the province, and that is the finding this paper would most like to be remembered for, because it applies to every study of this shape and not only to this one.

Data and code availability

The official emission factors are published by the Ministry of Ecology and Environment and the National Bureau of Statistics1. The downloaded source PDF, the extracted dataset, the analysis scripts and a provenance record giving the evidence level of every value used are held in the project repository. The analysis requires only the Python standard library; scripts/01_breakeven.py prints every number that appears in this paper so that a rerun can be checked against the text.
